\begin{abstract}
Biological neural circuits maintain functional computation despite substantial variation in synaptic strengths, membrane properties, and inputs. Reservoir computing provides an artificial framework for studying this robustness: input signals are projected into a high-dimensional space by a fixed nonlinear dynamical system, called the reservoir. Unlike biological circuits, these systems can be highly sensitive to hyperparameters such as spectral radius, input scaling, leak rate, and bias. This paper asks which reservoir-network features support invariance to those parameter changes. Using the \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome as a biologically grounded reservoir, we measure computational capability with four task-agnostic metrics---memory capacity (MC), information processing capacity (IPC), kernel rank (KR), and generalization rank (GR)---and define hyperparameter invariance as the coefficient of variation of these metrics across sweeps of spectral radius, input scaling, leak rate, and neuron bias.

To isolate contributors to invariance, we construct controlled perturbations that separately modify connectivity topology, weight-magnitude distributions, magnitude placement, and excitatory/inhibitory sign structure while preserving the remaining properties. Across these experiments, the anatomical connectome with predicted chemical polarity labels consistently occupies a relatively low-variance regime. 

The central result is a performance--invariance tradeoff: perturbations that raise task-agnostic computational capacity tend to make those capacities more dependent on reservoir hyperparameters. Across both sign-balance and shuffle controls, this tradeoff is closely tied to the raw spectral radius before normalization. Perturbations that lower the raw spectral radius are rescaled more strongly to the target radius and tend to increase both performance and CV, whereas perturbations that raise the raw spectral radius tend to decrease both. Thus, the EleganSign-predicted chemical sign structure is not interpreted as an optimum for reservoir performance; instead, it participates in a broader structural regime that favors lower hyperparameter sensitivity by shaping the spectrum of the recurrent matrix before normalization.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item The \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome occupies a low hyperparameter-sensitivity regime as a reservoir.
\item Higher task-agnostic capacity often coincides with higher sensitivity to reservoir hyperparameters.
\item Raw spectral radius before normalization mediates much of this tradeoff across sign and shuffle controls.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
Reservoir computing uses a fixed recurrent dynamical system to project input sequences into a high-dimensional state space, after which only a readout is trained \parencite{jaeger_echo_state_2001,maass_lsm_2002}. This makes reservoir models useful for studying how recurrent architecture shapes computation, but it also means dynamics and performance can vary strongly with hyperparameters such as spectral radius, input scaling, leak rate, and neuronal bias \parencite{Lukosevicius2012PracticalESN,Matzner2022HyperparameterTuningESN}.

Most reservoir-computing theory and practice treats random recurrent networks as a strong generic baseline because they can provide diverse, weakly correlated nonlinear features and be tuned near dynamical regimes that support memory and separability \parencite{Lukosevicius2012PracticalESN}.\raul{This is still a big jump} The \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome is a useful example: it is shaped by developmental, metabolic, behavioral, and stability constraints rather than by the goal of serving as an optimized random feature map.

This motivates a different question from peak-performance optimization: which structural properties make reservoir statistics stable across hyperparameter variation? The present study treats hyperparameter invariance as a metric of reservoir architecture.

Prior work has also used biological networks themselves as reservoirs or reservoir-like substrates. Kawai and colleagues evaluated echo-state networks with small-world structure and compared them to models with real human cortical connectivity \parencite{KAWAI201915}. Morra and Daley replaced the reservoir layer of an ESN with a fruit-fly connectome-derived topology, and later Morra and colleagues used a fruit-fly lateral-horn connectome to study multifunctionality in a connectome-based reservoir computer \parencite{morra_daley_2022,morra_fly_rc_2023}. In that later study, the fruit-fly connectome-based reservoir showed a broader window of \([\rho,\gamma]\)-space with multifunctionality and a larger magnitude of multifunctionality than the Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi control \parencite{morra_fly_rc_2023}. More recently, Iannello and colleagues described biological reservoir computing with cultured cortical neurons as the reservoir substrate \parencite{iannello_brc_2025}. Taken together, these studies show that both anatomical connectomes and living neuronal networks have been used as reservoirs, although they differ in whether the biological network is treated as a fixed graph or as an active physical substrate.

We use an anatomical \textit{C.\ elegans} chemical-synapse connectome with EleganSign-predicted chemical polarity labels and controlled perturbations that isolate topology, inferred excitatory/inhibitory sign structure, weight-magnitude distribution, magnitude placement, and sign placement. Reservoir behavior is evaluated with task-agnostic metrics, and hyperparameter invariance is quantified across sweeps of spectral radius, input scaling, leak rate, and neuron-bias range.

The results identify a performance--invariance tradeoff rather than a biological optimum. Perturbations that enhance computational performance often also increase sensitivity to hyperparameter choice, and the strongest effects appear to act through the raw spectral radius before normalization.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Reservoir model}
We use echo state networks, a discrete-time reservoir-computing model in which recurrent weights are fixed and only linear readouts trained using linear regression \parencite{jaeger_echo_state_2001,Lukosevicius2012PracticalESN}. With leak rate \(\alpha \in (0,1]\), the state update is
\begin{align}
\tilde{x}_{t+1} &= \tanh\!\left(W^{\mathrm{res}} x_t + s_{\mathrm{in}}W^{\mathrm{in}} u_{t+1} + b \right), \\
x_{t+1} &= (1-\alpha)\,x_t + \alpha\,\tilde{x}_{t+1}, \\
y_t &= W^{\mathrm{out}}\,x_t.
\end{align}
Here \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\) is the reservoir state at time \(t\), \(\tilde{x}_{t+1}\) is the state before leaky integration, \(y_t\) is the linear readout, \(W^{\mathrm{res}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\) is the sparse recurrent reservoir matrix, \(W^{\mathrm{in}}\) is the input matrix, \(s_{\mathrm{in}}\) scales the input drive, and \(b\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\) is a fixed per-neuron bias vector. \footnote{The readout dimension is \(p=5\) for IPC so that multiple polynomial orders can be solved in a single batch; otherwise \(p=1\).} Only \(W^{\mathrm{out}}\) is fit during metric calculations, and each instantiated reservoir is rescaled to a target spectral radius before evaluation.

\subsection{Reservoir hyperparameters}
The experiments sweep four hyperparameters: spectral radius, leak rate, input scaling, and neuron-bias. The spectral radius \(\rho(W^{\mathrm{res}})\) sets the overall strength of recurrent feedback, where
\[
\rho(W) = \max_{1 \le i \le N} |\lambda_i|.
\]
Here \(\lambda_i\) denotes the \(i\)th eigenvalue of \(W\). In reservoir computing, recurrent matrices are commonly rescaled to a target spectral radius near one to keep the dynamics in a useful operating range.\footnote{Reservoirs can remain computationally useful for values below or above this range \parencite{gallicchio_chasing_2019}.} In our experiments, we apply network perturbations then rescale $W$ to the target spectral radius. Let \(W'\) denote the signed recurrent matrix after all perturbations have been applied. For a target spectral radius \(\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\), the matrix is rescaled as
\[
W^{\mathrm{res}}
=
\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{target}}}{\rho(W')}W',
\qquad
\rho(W')=\max_i |\lambda_i(W')|.
\]

The leak rate \(\alpha\) sets the effective timescale of the reservoir, and input scaling \(s_{\mathrm{in}}\) sets the external drive. The neuron-bias \(\beta\) controls the fixed term \(b\) in the state update: when \(\beta>0\), each neuron receives an independently drawn bias \(b_i\sim \mathcal{U}(-\beta,\beta)\), which is held fixed for that reservoir instantiation.

\subsection{Task-agnostic reservoir metrics}
We measure four task-agnostic reservoir metrics: kernel rank (KR), generalization rank (GR), memory capacity (MC), and information processing capacity (IPC) \parencite{maass_comp_power_2004,Jaeger2002STMESN,dambre_ipc_2012,pilati_ceni_michieletti_gallicchio_ricciardi_milano_2026}.

\subsubsection{Kernel Rank}
Kernel rank measures how many effectively independent reservoir activity dimensions are produced by the input drive \parencite{maass_comp_power_2004}. We collect the post-washout \raul{define washout/post washout} state matrix \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times N}\), center it over time, compute singular values \(S=\{\sigma_i\}\), normalize them as \(p_i=\sigma_i/\sum_j\sigma_j\), and compute effective rank ($\mathrm{erank}$):
\[
\mathrm{KR} = \mathrm{erank}
=
\exp\left(-\sum_i p_i\ln p_i\right).
\]

\subsubsection{Generalization Rank}
Generalization rank measures how reservoir states change under small input perturbations \parencite{maass_comp_power_2004}. Let \(u_t\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)\) be the clean input and \(u'_t=u_t+\kappa\varepsilon_t\), where \(\varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(\kappa=0.01\). If \(X\) and \(X'\) are the corresponding post-washout state matrices, then
\[
\mathrm{GR}
=
\mathrm{erank}(X'-X).
\]

\subsubsection{Memory Capacity}
Memory capacity measures how well a linear readout can reconstruct past inputs from the current reservoir state \parencite{Jaeger2002STMESN}. For each delay \(d\), a ridge-regression readout is trained to predict \(u(t-d)\) from \(x(t)\), and the squared Pearson correlation between predicted and true delayed input is recorded. We approximate \raul{the} total memory capacity \raul{MC} by summing over delays:
\[
\mathrm{MC}
=
\sum_{d=1}^{D_{\max}}
r_{\mathrm{score}}(u(t-d),\hat{u}_d(t))^2.
\]

\subsubsection{Information Processing Capacity}
\label{sec:ipc}
Information processing capacity \raul{(IPC)}\miles{ I am not sure that this is needed because I already do it before} measures how well the reservoir can reconstruct nonlinear functions of delayed inputs \parencite{dambre_ipc_2012}. We use an incomplete, truncated single-delay IPC diagnostic rather than the full IPC decomposition. Specifically, we evaluate univariate Legendre polynomial targets of one delayed input at a time and omit pairwise and higher-order cross-delay product terms. This still measures the reservoir's ability to generate nonlinear transformations of input history, but it does not estimate the full capacity over all nonlinear input combinations. For Legendre polynomial orders \(K=\{1,2,3,4,5\}\), let \(z_{k,d}(t)=P_k(u(t-d))\). We compute
\[
\mathrm{IPC}
=
\sum_{d=1}^{D_{\max}}
\sum_{k\in K}
\max\!\left(0,\,
r_{\mathrm{score}}\!\left(z_{k,d}(t),\hat{z}_{k,d}(t)\right)^2
\right).
\]
Because cross-delay products are not included, the reported IPC values should be interpreted as an incomplete IPC-style statistic rather than an exhaustive estimate of full IPC.

\subsection{Hyperparameter-invariance metric}
To investigate stability under hyperparameter variation, we examine how each performance metric changes across the hyperparameter grid.
We quantify hyperparameter invariance with the coefficient of variation (CV):
\[CV = \frac{\text{std}(a)}{\bar{a}}\]
where \(a\) is the set of values for a given task-agnostic performance metric across all evaluated hyperparameter settings within a trial. CV is a measure of relative dispersion, which makes it useful for comparing variability across models with different performance scales. In reservoir computing, hyperparameter sensitivity is commonly analyzed through area-under-curve, performance curves, or robustness intervals rather than through a single standard statistic \parencite{KAWAI201915,jaurigue_timescale_2023,freddi_robust_spiking_2026,srinivasan_ei_balance_2025}. Our use of CV compresses sensitivity into one value per metric: low CV indicates that a metric remains stable across spectral radius, leak rate, input scaling, and neuron bias, whereas high CV indicates stronger dependence on hyperparameter choice.

\subsubsection{Connection shuffle}
\label{Connection-Shuffle}
We define a connection shuffle as a directed degree-preserving edge swap. Let \(W\) be the weighted adjacency matrix. We randomly select two existing directed edges \((a,b)\) and \((c,d)\), where \(a,b,c,d\) are distinct and the proposed edges \((a,d)\) and \((c,b)\) do not already exist. We then form a new matrix \(W'\) by setting
\[
W'_{ad}=W_{ab}, \qquad
W'_{cb}=W_{cd}, \qquad
W'_{ab}=0, \qquad
W'_{cd}=0,
\]
with all other entries unchanged. This preserves each node's in-degree and out-degree while shuffling the neighborhood structure.
\subsubsection{Weight shuffle}

\label{Weight-Shuffle}
Fix the synaptic connectivity pattern and randomly permute the weights assigned to existing connections. Let
\[
NZ=\{(i,j): W_{ij}\neq 0\}
\]
denote the set of nonzero entries in the weight matrix. We extract the corresponding weights into a vector,
\[
w_{NZ}=\{W_{ij}:(i,j)\in NZ\},
\]
shuffle this vector, and then reassign the shuffled weights back to the same nonzero indices. Thus, the set of existing connections is unchanged, but the weights assigned to those connections are redistributed across the fixed network topology.

\subsection{Shuffle controls}
\label{sec:shuf}
The shuffle controls change where edges, signs, or magnitudes are placed. Connection shuffles rewire directed edges while preserving in-degree and out-degree. Weight shuffles keep the connectivity fixed but permute the nonzero magnitudes. Connection-plus-weight shuffles apply both operations. Sign shuffles keep the global sign ratio fixed but permute which edges carry positive or negative signs.

Figure~\ref{fig:shuf_all} shows these controls in the same mean-performance and CV plane used above. Columns correspond to GR, IPC, KR, and MC. Rows separate three baseline weight regimes: all-positive binary weights on the top row, signed-unit weights in the middle row, and real-valued \textit{C.\ elegans} contact-count weights on the bottom row. Contour color gives the percent change in raw spectral radius relative to the row baseline, with blue indicating lower \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) and red indicating higher \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\).

In the real-valued row, the shuffle controls move away from the unshuffled predicted-polarity reservoir toward higher mean performance and higher CV. \subsection{Raw spectral radius summarizes the performance--invariance tradeoff}
\label{sec:raw_rho_summary}
The sign-normalization and shuffle results point to the same mechanism: structural perturbations change the raw recurrent spectrum before spectral-radius normalization, and this changes the effective recurrent scale after normalization. Figure~\ref{fig:raw_rho_summary} summarizes this relationship across the sign-fraction sweeps and the shuffle controls. Across metrics, lower \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) is generally associated with higher mean performance and higher CV, while higher \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) is associated with lower performance and lower CV.

This summary should not be read as saying that raw spectral radius is an independent architectural feature separate from topology, sign placement, or weight magnitudes. Rather, those structural features appear to alter performance by changing \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\). The plotted families show that the same direction appears in multiple experimental settings: balanced sign sweeps, signed-unit controls, binary topology controls, and real-weight shuffles. Thus, the synthesis is not simply that the global E/I ratio matters. A purely sign-balance explanation would predict that controls with the same negative-edge fraction should behave similarly. Instead, the shuffle and normalization experiments show that topology, local sign placement, weight-magnitude distribution and placement can move reservoirs with the same global sign ratio into different performance--CV regimes. These factors appear to act through a shared spectral pathway: they change the raw recurrent spectrum before normalization, which then changes the effective recurrent scale after spectral-radius rescaling.

\section{Discussion}
We find a hyperparameter invariance--performance tradeoff dictated by how network structure changes the raw recurrent spectrum before normalization. The anatomical \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome with predicted chemical polarity labels occupies a relatively low-variance reservoir regime, and the ablations point to an interwoven hierarchy of causes. Global E/I sign imbalance is the strongest structural perturbation in the model studied here: preserving the EleganSign-inferred chemical sign ratio recovers much of the connectome's invariance even when exact sign placement and contact-count weight magnitudes are altered. Sign balance, local sign placement, topology, and magnitude placement change \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\); spectral-radius normalization then converts those raw spectral differences into different effective recurrent weight scales.

Moving toward a more balanced E/I regime generally increases mean task-agnostic performance, but it also increases CV across spectral radius, leak rate, input scaling, and neuron bias. The same pattern appears in the shuffle controls: manipulations that lower \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) tend to move reservoirs toward higher performance and higher CV, while manipulations that raise \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) tend to move them toward lower performance and lower CV. Under the present modeling choices the anatomical \textit{C.\ elegans} instantiation with predicted chemical polarity labels lies on the low-variance side of this curve rather than at the peak-performance region. Put more directly, the approximately 4:1 excitatory-to-inhibitory balance associated with many biological networks does not prioritize peak reservoir performance in this abstraction; it helps produce a more hyperparameter-invariant, lower-performance operating point by maintaining a raw spectrum that does not require as much post-normalization amplification.


Direct biological interpretation should be cautious because the connectome model compresses substantial uncertainty into a static network with predicted chemical polarity labels. The main analyses use EleganSign-derived labels for chemical synapses, but these labels are inferred rather than directly measured: they come from gene-expression and neurotransmitter logic, not from voltage- or calcium-response measurements \parencite{fenyves_szilágyi_zsolt_vassy_csaba_2020,elegansign_web}. EleganSign also does not solve gap-junction signs, since gap junctions are electrical couplings that are usually modeled as undirected or symmetric in anatomical connectome work. Coverage is incomplete as well: many chemical synapses have unknown, complex, or unpredicted labels, and the inferred inhibitory edge fraction depends on how those ambiguous cases are handled. We therefore analyze both a predicted-polarity-only version that removes those ambiguous edges and an augmented version that assigns them random signs under a 4:1 excitatory-to-inhibitory edge prior. If the true inhibitory edge fraction differs from either approximation, the anatomical connectome with predicted chemical polarity labels may lie elsewhere along the observed performance--CV curve. In addition, E/I balance varies across development and aging \parencite{wirak_florman_alkema_connor_gabel_2022, tanis_bellemer_moresco_forbush_koelle_2009}, and our model delivers inputs to all neurons rather than to biologically separated populations--such as sending inputs to input neurons and taking outputs from motor neurons. The present work should therefore be read as a structural reservoir analysis, not a faithful dynamical simulation of the animal.

\subsection{Shape of the sign-balance curves}

The approximate symmetry of the sign-fraction curves around a balanced edge-sign ratio is also consistent with the mathematical form of the reservoir used here. The state nonlinearity is \(\tanh\), which is odd-symmetric, and the readout is linear. Under these choices, exchanging positive recurrent weights for negative recurrent weights changes the sign structure of the recurrent drive, but the linear readout can absorb corresponding sign changes in the state features. This makes the sign-flip experiment approximately symmetric. The centered peak near 50:50 edge signs is therefore consistent with balanced signs suppressing coherent recurrent feedback loops, lowering \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\), and increasing the post-normalization effective recurrent scale. A centered peak in performance and a corresponding increase in CV near balanced edge signs are natural for this abstraction.

This helps explain why the present curves differ from studies in which the best operating point is slightly inhibition-dominated. Srinivasan, Plenz, and Girvan report performance benefits from balanced or slightly inhibition-dominated adaptive E/I regimes, while de Graaf and colleagues use a rectified \(\tanh\)-like activation in a locomotor circuit model \parencite{srinivasan_ei_balance_2025,de_graaf_spinal_inhibition_2025}. In such models, the activation function is not fully sign-symmetric: positive and negative inputs are transformed differently, so the optimal E/I ratio can shift away from exactly 50:50. By contrast, the odd activation and linear readout used here make the sign-flipping experiment more symmetric by construction. The centered shape of the curves should therefore be interpreted as a property of this reservoir abstraction, not as a claim that biological circuits should generally optimize at equal excitation and inhibition.


For reservoir computing, the practical implication is that raw spectral radius before normalization should be treated as a key mediator between architecture and performance--invariance behavior. E/I sign ratio remains an important design parameter, complementing recent performance-focused work on adaptive E/I control in reservoirs \parencite{srinivasan_ei_balance_2025}, but its effect should be evaluated through the spectrum it induces together with topology, sign placement, magnitude distribution, and magnitude placement.

\subsection{Future directions}

Several extensions would help clarify these interpretations. First, the model comparisons could be strengthened by adding additional topology controls that match degree and sign statistics more tightly, including degree-matched random and scale-free networks. Such controls would help determine which residual effects are genuinely topological and whether the distinctive sensitivity of MC persists outside the \textit{C.\ elegans} degree distribution.

Second, the present conclusions should be tested on downstream tasks such as NARMA to determine whether the same structural relationships hold when reservoirs are evaluated by task performance rather than task-agnostic statistics alone. The same framework could also be used to investigate how connectome optimization algorithms \parencite{churchland_garcia-ojalvo_2026} alter task-agnostic statistics, and whether task-driven optimization moves biological reservoirs along the same performance--invariance axes identified here. Extending the ablation grid across additional topologies, magnitude distributions, and sign-placement rules would also test how far the relative importance of E/I imbalance generalizes beyond the present connectome-centered setting.

Third, it would be valuable to repeat these experiments with biologically separated input and output populations. Using sensory neurons as inputs and muscles or motor neurons as outputs would bring the model closer to a functional circuit interpretation and would better connect this work to prior studies of connectome-based computation \parencite{KAWAI201915,yu_bpu_2025}. Finally, the role of sign inference deserves more direct study. Natural next steps would be to compare alternative neurotransmitter-to-sign mappings and to repeat the sign-balance analyses under inferred inhibitory fractions closer to those suggested by recent biological work.

\[

\]
then a random subset of size \(N_-\) of nonzero entries is assigned sign \(-1\), and all remaining nonzero entries are assigned sign \(+1\). This preserves the global E/I sign ratio while shuffling sign placement.

This ablation preserves the biological connectivity pattern but removes both sign and magnitude heterogeneity:

\begin{cases}

0 & \text{if } W_{ij} = 0.
\end{cases}
\]

\subsubsection{C.~elegans topology with Gaussian weights}
This control keeps the biological connectivity pattern but replaces each nonzero weight with an independent Gaussian sample:
\[
W'_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
X_{ij} & \text{if } W_{ij} \neq 0,\\
0 & \text{if } W_{ij} = 0,
\end{cases}

\]

\subsubsection{C.~elegans--matched Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi model with Gaussian weights}

\subsubsection{Watts--Strogatz Gaussian control}

\subsubsection{C. elegans architecture with uniform weights using original connection signs}

\subsection{Neuroscience Terms Explained for Computer Scientists}
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\textbf{E/I balance} -- the ratio or fraction of excitatory to inhibitory synaptic signs in the modeled reservoir; in this paper this is an edge-level quantity, not a count of excitatory or inhibitory neurons}
    \item {\textbf{neurotransmitter} -- a chemical that allows neurons to communicate with each other by transmitting signals across synapses \parencite{Sheffler2023Neurotransmitters}.}
    \item {\textbf{gap junction} -- an electrical connection between adjacent cells, usually modeled as an undirected or symmetric coupling. EleganSign does not assign chemical polarity labels to gap junctions; when gap junctions are included in reservoir experiments, they should be modeled separately from chemical synapses.}
    \item {\textbf{neuromuscular junction} -- a specialized synapse where a motor neuron communicates with a muscle fiber. These directed edges point to non-neural cells and hence are not included in our graph.}
    \item {\textbf{synaptic terminals} -- bulbous endings of a neuron's axon where neurotransmitters are transferred to other cells (neurons or muscles). We use the number of these terminals as a proxy for edge weight.}
    \item {\textbf{membrane potential} -- the difference in electrical charge between the inside and outside of a neuron, forming the basis for neural activity \parencite{Henley_2021}. For our purposes, this is the internal state of the neuron representing how much our activation function will be saturated.}
    \item {\textbf{small-worldness} -- a property commonly observed in biological networks. A graph that exhibits the small-world property has both a high clustering coefficient and a short average path length. This structure is claimed to make information spread more efficient and to make the network more robust \parencite{Bassett2007,latora2001}.}
    \item {\textbf{scale-free network}} -- a network whose degree distribution follows a power law, meaning the fraction of nodes with degree $k$ follows a power law $k^{-\alpha}$ \parencite{broido_clauset_2019}.
\end{itemize}
