\begin{abstract}
Biological neural circuits maintain functional computation despite substantial variation in synaptic strengths, membrane properties, and inputs. Reservoir computing provides an artificial framework for studying this robustness: input signals are projected into a high-dimensional space by a fixed nonlinear dynamical system, called the reservoir. Unlike biological circuits, these systems can be highly sensitive to hyperparameters such as spectral radius, input scaling, leak rate, and bias. This paper asks which reservoir-network features support invariance to those parameter changes. Using the \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome as a biologically grounded reservoir, we measure computational capability with four task-agnostic metrics---memory capacity (MC), information processing capacity (IPC), kernel rank (KR), and generalization rank (GR)---and define hyperparameter invariance as the coefficient of variation of these metrics across sweeps of spectral radius, input scaling, leak rate, and neuron bias.

To isolate contributors to invariance, we construct controlled perturbations that separately modify connectivity topology, weight-magnitude distributions, magnitude placement, and excitatory/inhibitory sign structure while preserving the remaining properties. Across these experiments, the empirical connectome consistently occupies a relatively low-variance regime. 

The central result is a performance--invariance tradeoff: perturbations that raise task-agnostic computational capacity tend to make those capacities more dependent on reservoir hyperparameters. Across both sign-balance and shuffle controls, this tradeoff is closely tied to the raw spectral radius before normalization. Perturbations that lower the raw spectral radius are rescaled more strongly to the target radius and tend to increase both performance and CV, whereas perturbations that raise the raw spectral radius tend to decrease both. Thus, the biological sign structure is not interpreted as an optimum for reservoir performance; instead, it participates in a broader structural regime that favors lower hyperparameter sensitivity by shaping the spectrum of the recurrent matrix before normalization.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item The \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome occupies a low hyperparameter-sensitivity regime as a reservoir.
\item Higher task-agnostic capacity often coincides with higher sensitivity to reservoir hyperparameters.
\item Raw spectral radius before normalization mediates much of this tradeoff across sign and shuffle controls.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
Reservoir computing uses a fixed recurrent dynamical system to project input histories into a high-dimensional state space, after which only a readout is trained \parencite{jaeger_echo_state_2001,maass_lsm_2002}. This separation makes reservoir models useful for studying how recurrent architecture shapes computation. It also exposes a persistent design problem: reservoir behavior can depend strongly on hyperparameters such as spectral radius, input scaling, leak rate, and neuronal bias \raul{citation needed}. A reservoir that performs well only under a narrow set of choices is less useful as a robust computational substrate.

Most reservoir-computing theory and practice treats random recurrent networks as a strong generic baseline. Random reservoirs can provide diverse, weakly correlated nonlinear features and can be tuned near dynamical regimes that support memory and separability \parencite{Lukosevicius2012PracticalESN}. From that perspective, a small, sparse, structured, signed biological circuit should not be expected to maximize generic task-agnostic capacity. \raul{you can make this transition a bit more smooth. Like: The C. elegans connectome provides a concrete instance of this. As a mapped nervous system, it has been shaped by evolutionary demands that tightly constrain its size, sparsity etc...} The \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome was shaped by developmental, metabolic, behavioral, and stability constraints, not by the objective of serving as an optimized random feature map. Its value here is therefore not as a presumed best reservoir, but as a structured biological prior for asking which constraints change the relationship between performance and sensitivity.

This distinction matters because biological neural systems often remain functional across variable internal and environmental conditions, whereas artificial systems can be brittle outside the regime in which they were tuned or trained \parencite{recht2019,geirhos_jacobsen_michaelis_zemel_brendel_bethge_wichmann_2020}. In reservoir computing terms, this suggests a question that is different from peak-performance optimization: which structural properties make computational statistics stable across hyperparameter variation? The present study addresses that question by treating hyperparameter invariance as a metric of reservoir architectures.

Prior work has also used biological networks themselves as reservoirs or reservoir-like substrates. Kawai and colleagues evaluated echo-state networks with small-world structure and compared them to models with real human cortical connectivity \parencite{KAWAI201915}. Morra and Daley replaced the reservoir layer of an ESN with a fruit-fly connectome-derived topology, and later Morra and colleagues used a fruit-fly lateral-horn connectome to study multifunctionality in a connectome-based reservoir computer \parencite{morra_daley_2022,morra_fly_rc_2023}. In that later study, the fruit-fly connectome-based reservoir showed a broader window of \([\rho,\gamma]\)-space with multifunctionality and a larger magnitude of multifunctionality than the Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi control \parencite{morra_fly_rc_2023}. More recently, Iannello and colleagues described biological reservoir computing with cultured cortical neurons as the reservoir substrate \parencite{iannello_brc_2025}. Taken together, these studies show that both anatomical connectomes and living neuronal networks have been used as reservoirs, although they differ in whether the biological network is treated as a fixed graph or as an active physical substrate.

We use the signed chemical-synapse \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome as a biologically grounded reservoir and compare it with controlled perturbations that isolate topology, excitatory/inhibitory sign structure, weight-magnitude distribution, magnitude placement, and sign placement. Reservoir behavior is evaluated with four task-agnostic metrics: memory capacity (MC), information processing capacity (IPC), kernel rank (KR), and generalization rank (GR). Hyperparameter invariance is quantified as the coefficient of variation of these metrics across a full factorial sweep of spectral radius, input scaling, leak rate, and neuron-bias range.

The results identify a performance--invariance tradeoff rather than a biological optimum for reservoir performance. Perturbations that increase task-agnostic capacity often also increase sensitivity to hyperparameter choice. Global excitatory/inhibitory sign imbalance strongly influences the low-variance regime observed in the \textit{C.\ elegans} model studied here, but it is not a universal determinant acting alone. Its effect depends on topology, local sign placement, weight-magnitude distribution, and magnitude placement because these structural features change the raw spectral radius that is subsequently normalized during reservoir evaluation.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Reservoir model}
We use echo state networks, a discrete-time reservoir-computing model in which recurrent weights are fixed and only linear readouts are fit when a metric requires reconstruction \parencite{jaeger_echo_state_2001,Lukosevicius2012PracticalESN}. Let \(u_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) be the input, \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N}\) the reservoir state, and \(y_t \in \mathbb{R}^{p}\) the readout. With leak rate \(\alpha \in (0,1]\), the state update is
\begin{align}
\tilde{x}_{t+1} &= \tanh\!\left(W^{\mathrm{res}} x_t + s_{\mathrm{in}}W^{\mathrm{in}} u_{t+1} + b + \xi_t\right), \\
x_{t+1} &= (1-\alpha)\,x_t + \alpha\,\tilde{x}_{t+1}, \\
y_t &= W^{\mathrm{out}}\,x_t.
\end{align}
Here \(W^{\mathrm{res}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\) is the sparse recurrent reservoir matrix, \(W^{\mathrm{in}}\) is the input matrix, \(s_{\mathrm{in}}\) scales the input drive, \(b\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\) is a fixed per-neuron bias vector, and \(\xi_t\) denotes optional noise. Noise is not included in the experiments. The readout dimension is \(p=5\) for IPC so that multiple polynomial orders can be solved in a single batch; otherwise \(p=1\).

Only \(W^{\mathrm{out}}\) is fit during metric calculations. The recurrent matrix is fixed after the architecture is instantiated, so the experiments isolate how reservoir structure and global hyperparameters shape the state matrix available to the readout. Architectures are rescaled to a target spectral radius before each trial is evaluated, as described below. This keeps the comparison focused on relative structure rather than raw matrix scale.

\subsection{Reservoir hyperparameters}
The experiments sweep four hyperparameters that control the reservoir's operating regime. The spectral radius \(\rho(W^{\mathrm{res}})\) controls the strength of recurrent feedback, where
\[
\rho(W) = \max_{1 \le i \le N} |\lambda_i|.
\]
This target is imposed after each architecture or perturbation has been instantiated. Let \(W'\) denote the signed recurrent matrix after all topology, sign, and magnitude manipulations have been applied. For a target spectral radius \(\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\), the matrix used during the trial is
\[
W^{\mathrm{res}}
=
\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{target}}}{\rho(W')}W',
\qquad
\rho(W')=\max_i |\lambda_i(W')|.
\]
Thus, spectral-radius scaling is the final matrix operation before the reservoir is driven by the input sequence.

This normalization is important because otherwise architecture comparisons could be dominated by raw weight scale rather than by topology, sign balance, or magnitude placement. Matching every instantiated matrix to the same target spectral radius makes the hyperparameter sweep compare reservoirs at matched recurrent gain values. Normalization does not erase the effect of structure: topology and sign placement determine the raw spectrum of \(W'\), and therefore determine the scale factor required to reach \(\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\). For example, sign-balanced matrices can cancel reinforcing loops and change the leading eigenvalue structure, while strongly sign-imbalanced matrices tend to preserve more coherent positive feedback. The resulting normalization factor changes the effective scale of recurrent weights used during trials.

The leak rate \(\alpha\) controls the effective timescale of the reservoir by interpolating between the previous state and the newly computed activation. Input scaling \(s_{\mathrm{in}}\) controls the strength of the external drive relative to recurrent feedback. The neuron-bias half-range \(\beta\) controls each neuron's baseline operating point: when \(\beta=0\), \(b=0\); when \(\beta>0\), each neuron is sampled independently as \(b_i\sim\mathcal{U}(-\beta,\beta)\) and then held fixed during trials. Positive bias values raise a neuron's baseline activation tendency, while negative values lower it.

These parameters do not alter the trained readout directly; they change the dynamics of the reservoir whose stability is evaluated by the invariance analysis.

\subsection{Connectome data and preprocessing}
\label{sec:connectome_dataset}
A connectome is a description of the neural wiring of an organism. It encodes which neurons are connected, the direction of each connection, and often additional biological metadata such as the number of synaptic terminals and the neurotransmitter associated with each connection.

In this work, we treat the connectome as the foundation for a graph: each neuron is represented as a node, and each synapse as a directed, weighted edge. Two \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome sources are used. The main experiments use the signed chemical-synapse connectome derived from EleganSign \parencite{fenyves_szilágyi_zsolt_vassy_csaba_2020,elegansign_web}. We analyze two preprocessing versions of this dataset. In the signed-only version, unknown or complex-sign connections are removed, leaving 297 nodes and 1740 directed signed edges. In the augmented random-sign version, the 1864 unknown or complex-sign edges are retained and assigned signs randomly with a 4:1 excitatory-to-inhibitory ratio. The older c302-derived connectome \parencite{c302} is retained only for the supplemental architecture visualizations and legacy schematic controls; it is not the source of the main results figures.

The signed-only graph contains 1740 directed chemical-synapse edges over 297 neurons after preprocessing. These signs are treated as the empirical excitatory/inhibitory partition for the invariance experiments. Throughout this paper, excitatory and inhibitory refer to signed connections, not to neuron classes: a 50\% inhibitory condition means that 50\% of modeled edges have negative sign, not that 50\% of neurons are inhibitory. The augmented random-sign graph adds the 1864 unknown or complex-sign connections back into the topology and assigns each such edge a random sign under a 4:1 excitatory-to-inhibitory prior. This assignment is used as a sensitivity analysis rather than as a claim that those individual signs are known. Both versions remain \emph{working abstractions}: they do not attempt to capture receptor-specific, context-dependent, developmental, or neuromodulatory effects. Because this study examines how coarse sign structure and weight statistics influence task-agnostic reservoir metrics across hyperparameter sweeps, incorporating detailed synapse-type dynamics is beyond the scope of the analysis and computationally expensive at the current scope of our experiments.

When edge magnitudes are required, the number of synaptic contacts from neuron \(i\) to neuron \(j\) is used as a weight proxy, \(W_{ij}=c_{ij}\). Treating contact number as a proxy weight is standard in \emph{C.\ elegans} modeling \parencite{c302,Casal2020,churchland_garcia-ojalvo_2026} and provides the biologically informed magnitude prior for the reservoir.

\subsection{Task-agnostic reservoir metrics}
Task-agnostic reservoir statistics measure computational properties of the reservoir before training it on a specific downstream task. This study uses four such metrics: kernel rank (KR), generalization rank (GR), memory capacity (MC), and information processing capacity (IPC), following prior work on reservoir/neural-microcircuit diagnostics and benchmarking \parencite{maass_comp_power_2004,Jaeger2002STMESN,dambre_ipc_2012,pilati_ceni_michieletti_gallicchio_ricciardi_milano_2026}. For each architecture and hyperparameter setting, the reservoir is driven by an i.i.d. input sequence and the resulting state matrix is used to compute these metrics. Higher MC, IPC, and KR indicate greater task-agnostic computational capability. GR measures sensitivity to small input perturbations; throughout the results it is analyzed alongside the other metrics as a reservoir response statistic.

\subsubsection{Kernel Rank}
Kernel rank measures how many effectively independent reservoir activity dimensions are produced by the input drive \parencite{maass_comp_power_2004}. We collect the post-washout state matrix \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times N}\), center it over time, compute singular values \(S=\{\sigma_i\}\), normalize them as \(p_i=\sigma_i/\sum_j\sigma_j\), and compute effective rank:
\[
\mathrm{KR} = \mathrm{erank}
=
\exp\left(-\sum_i p_i\ln p_i\right).
\]
This effective-rank formulation captures the spread of activity across orthogonal modes rather than exact linear independence alone \parencite{roy_effective_rank_2007}.

\subsubsection{Generalization Rank}
Generalization rank measures how reservoir states change under small input perturbations \parencite{maass_comp_power_2004}. Let \(u_t\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)\) be the clean input and \(u'_t=u_t+\kappa\varepsilon_t\), where \(\varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(\kappa=0.01\). If \(X\) and \(X'\) are the corresponding post-washout state matrices, then
\[
\mathrm{GR}
=
\mathrm{erank}(X'-X).
\]

\subsubsection{Memory Capacity}
Memory capacity measures how well a linear readout can reconstruct past inputs from the current reservoir state \parencite{Jaeger2002STMESN}. For each delay \(d\), a ridge-regression readout is trained to predict \(u(t-d)\) from \(x(t)\), and the squared Pearson correlation between predicted and true delayed input is recorded. We approximate total memory capacity by summing over delays:
\[
\mathrm{MC}
=
\sum_{d=1}^{D_{\max}}
r_{\mathrm{score}}(u(t-d),\hat{u}_d(t))^2.
\]
We use \(D_{\max}=30\). Validation sweeps over \(D_{\max}\in\{30,50,80\}\) showed that this cutoff preserved essentially the full measured MC in the models tested.

\subsubsection{Information Processing Capacity}
\label{sec:ipc}
Information processing capacity measures how well the reservoir can reconstruct nonlinear functions of delayed inputs \parencite{dambre_ipc_2012}. We use an incomplete, truncated single-delay IPC diagnostic rather than the full IPC decomposition. Specifically, we evaluate univariate Legendre polynomial targets of one delayed input at a time and omit pairwise and higher-order cross-delay product terms. This still measures the reservoir's ability to generate nonlinear transformations of input history, but it does not estimate the full capacity over all nonlinear input combinations. For Legendre polynomial orders \(K=\{1,2,3,4,5\}\), let \(z_{k,d}(t)=P_k(u(t-d))\). We compute
\[
\mathrm{IPC}
=
\sum_{d=1}^{D_{\max}}
\sum_{k\in K}
\max\!\left(0,\,
r_{\mathrm{score}}\!\left(z_{k,d}(t),\hat{z}_{k,d}(t)\right)^2
\right).
\]
We use \(D_{\max}=30\). Validation against longer delay windows and univariate polynomial orders \(1\) through \(10\) showed that this incomplete single-delay IPC closely matched the larger single-delay computation while reducing runtime. Because cross-delay products are not included, the reported IPC values should be interpreted as an incomplete IPC-style statistic for nonlinear input transformations rather than as an exhaustive estimate of full IPC.





\subsection{Hyperparameter-invariance metric}
We quantify hyperparameter invariance by measuring how much each task-agnostic metric changes across the hyperparameter grid. Because MC, IPC, KR, and GR have different numerical scales, we use the coefficient of variation (CV) rather than raw variance:

\[CV = \frac{\text{std}(a)}{\bar{a}}\]

where \(a\) is the set of values for a metric across the evaluated hyperparameter settings within a trial. Low CV indicates that a metric is stable under hyperparameter variation; it does not by itself imply high absolute performance. The results therefore analyze CV together with mean metric value.

The small-world model belongs to the random-topology baseline family rather than to the perturbation operators below. Small-world networks combine relatively high clustering with short path lengths \parencite{network_science}. They are included because prior reservoir-computing work found that small-world reservoirs can maintain memory performance across a broader range of spectral radii than fully random reservoirs when input and output nodes are spatially segregated \parencite{KAWAI201915,Wssmallworld}. In this study, the small-world network is used only as a topology control: it tests whether a generic clustered-and-short-path architecture reproduces the invariance profile of the \textit{C.\ elegans} reservoir once empirical signs and magnitudes are removed.

\subsubsection{Connection shuffle}
\label{Connection-Shuffle}
We define a connection shuffle as a directed degree-preserving edge swap. Let \(W\) be the weighted adjacency matrix. We randomly select two existing directed edges \((a,b)\) and \((c,d)\), where \(a,b,c,d\) are distinct and the proposed edges \((a,d)\) and \((c,b)\) do not already exist. We then form a new matrix \(W'\) by setting
\[
W'_{ad}=W_{ab}, \qquad
W'_{cb}=W_{cd}, \qquad
W'_{ab}=0, \qquad
W'_{cd}=0,
\]
with all other entries unchanged. In this operation, the weights are carried with the original edges as they are reassigned to new targets. This preserves each node's in-degree and out-degree while shuffling the neighborhood structure.
\subsubsection{Weight shuffle}

\label{Weight-Shuffle}
Fix the synaptic connectivity pattern and randomly permute the weights assigned to existing connections. Let
\[
NZ=\{(i,j): W_{ij}\neq 0\}
\]
denote the set of nonzero entries in the weight matrix. We extract the corresponding weights into a vector,
\[
w_{NZ}=\{W_{ij}:(i,j)\in NZ\},
\]
randomly shuffle this vector using \texttt{numpy.random.shuffle}, and then reassign the shuffled weights back to the same nonzero indices. Thus, the set of existing connections is unchanged, but the weights assigned to those connections are redistributed across the fixed network topology.

For this shuffle, the probability that a given connection changes sign depends on the empirical sign ratio of the graph being shuffled. This operation is therefore not sign-preserving unless explicitly stated otherwise.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\textit{C.\ elegans} reservoirs are relatively insensitive to reservoir hyperparameter choices, but the experiments below do not support a flat list of equally important or independent causes. In the present model, global excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) sign imbalance is the strongest single control we identify: preserving the empirical sign ratio recovers much of the connectome's invariance for IPC, KR, and GR, even when exact sign placement or empirical magnitudes are altered or simplified. This should not be read as a universal claim about E/I balance alone. The relative effect of sign imbalance depends on the surrounding architecture, including topology, sign placement, weight-magnitude distribution, and magnitude placement.

\subsection[The empirical connectome occupies a low-variance reservoir regime]{The empirical connectome occupies a low-variance reservoir regime}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} establishes the basic phenomenon. Across sweeps of spectral radius, leak rate, input scaling, and neuron bias, the empirical \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome lies in a low-CV regime for the four task-agnostic metrics considered here. Low CV means that the metric changes relatively little as hyperparameters vary, so this regime corresponds to hyperparameter invariance rather than high absolute performance.

The architecture comparison gives an initial ordering of possible causes within this experimental family. Models that do not preserve the empirical sign structure, shown in yellow, occupy the highest-CV region and generally move toward higher mean performance. The maximally imbalanced binary-weight control occupies the lowest-CV, lowest-performance island. By contrast, sign-preserving models remain closer to the empirical connectome, even when their nonzero weight magnitudes are simplified or altered. This suggests that weight magnitudes and their placement contribute to invariance in the \textit{C.\ elegans} reservoir model, but their effects are smaller than the effect of preserving the empirical E/I sign structure. The performance--CV island plot makes this ordering visible in the joint space of mean metric value and hyperparameter sensitivity: the empirical connectome occupies a compact low-CV island, while balanced random-sign controls move toward higher performance and higher CV. These comparisons motivate the central claim tested in the following sections: global E/I sign imbalance is the strongest contributor to the connectome's low-variance behavior under the present topology and magnitude regimes, while sign placement and weight magnitudes have smaller effects.

\subsection[Global sign balance reveals a performance-invariance tradeoff]{Global sign balance reveals a performance--invariance tradeoff}
\label{balance}
To isolate global sign balance, we flipped the signs of randomly selected synapses while preserving the remaining structure of each network. This manipulation changes the inhibitory edge fraction while holding connectivity and magnitudes fixed. It does not assign neurons to excitatory or inhibitory classes. The result is a clear performance--invariance tradeoff. In the all-positive or strongly sign-imbalanced regime, reservoirs have low CV but reduced mean computational performance. As the fraction of negative edges increases toward a more balanced E/I edge-sign regime, mean metric values generally rise, but CV rises as well (Figure~\ref{fig:bin_cel_frac_curves}). The same qualitative trend appears in the \textit{C.\ elegans}-matched ER topology and in the unknown/complex-removed \textit{C.\ elegans} magnitude pattern (Supplemental Figures~\ref{fig:er_frac_curves} and~\ref{fig:og_cel_frac_curves}). Thus, the same manipulation that increases computational capability also makes the reservoir more sensitive to spectral radius, leak rate, input scaling, and neuron bias.

This performance trend is consistent with recent reservoir studies in which balanced or slightly inhibition-dominated regimes outperform excitation-dominated regimes on memory, prediction, and locomotor-pattern tasks \parencite{srinivasan_ei_balance_2025,de_graaf_spinal_inhibition_2025}. The present result extends that framing by emphasizing not only mean performance, but also the accompanying increase in hyperparameter sensitivity.

This pattern explains why preserving the empirical sign ratio recovers much of the connectome's invariance in the architecture comparison. The empirical connectome, under the coarse neurotransmitter-to-sign mapping used here, sits on the low-variance side of the tradeoff rather than near the peak-performance region. In the reservoir-computing abstraction studied here, the approximately 4:1 excitatory-to-inhibitory balance associated with many biological networks does not prioritize peak task-agnostic performance. Instead, it places the reservoir in a lower-performance but more hyperparameter-invariant regime. Thus global E/I sign imbalance is a strong control parameter in this model, but its effect is expressed through the tested topology and magnitude regimes.

\subsection[Sign-normalization links sign balance to spectral-radius scaling]{Sign-normalization links sign balance to spectral-radius scaling}
\label{sec:sign_norm_ablation}
The sign-fraction curves can be interpreted through the way edge-sign balance changes the raw spectral radius before normalization. Changing the fraction of negative edges changes whether recurrent loops reinforce each other or cancel. More negative edges can reduce the raw spectral radius by disrupting coherent positive feedback. When each perturbed matrix is normalized by its own spectral radius, \(W/\rho(W)\), a lower raw \(\rho(W)\) produces a larger scale factor for the same target spectral radius. In other words, sign balance can indirectly allow larger effective recurrent weights after normalization.

The sign-normalization ablations test this mechanism by comparing the usual own-radius normalization, \(W/\rho(W)\), with normalization by the original matrix's spectral radius, \(W/\rho(W_{\mathrm{orig}})\). Under own-radius normalization, performance increases strongly as the negative edge fraction moves toward the balanced regime, and CV increases at the same time (Figure~\ref{fig:sign_norm_matched_cel}). When the same perturbed matrices are instead scaled by the original spectral radius, the performance curves are much flatter through the middle of the sign-fraction sweep. The ER-matched and unknown/complex-removed controls show the same qualitative separation between normalization conventions (Supplemental Figures~\ref{fig:sign_norm_matched_er} and~\ref{fig:sign_norm_removed_cel}).

This flattening is expected only over the range of sign fractions where the perturbed matrix has a lower raw spectral radius than the original matrix. Let \(q\) denote the negative edge fraction and \(q_{\mathrm{orig}}\) the original fraction. For the sign-sweep experiments, this middle region is approximately the interval between \(q_{\mathrm{orig}}\) and its sign-complement \(1-q_{\mathrm{orig}}\). In that range, \(\rho(W)<\rho(W_{\mathrm{orig}})\), so original-radius scaling gives a post-scaling radius
\[
\rho_{\mathrm{post}}
=
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}\frac{\rho(W)}{\rho(W_{\mathrm{orig}})}
<
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}.
\]
Though the weight magnitudes are not changing, the post-normalization spectral radius is. In all three experiments, performance stays flat in these inner fractions. Outside this middle range, the perturbed matrix can have \(\rho(W)>\rho(W_{\mathrm{orig}})\). Scaling by \(\rho(W_{\mathrm{orig}})\) then moves the post-scaling spectral radius back toward, or above, the target value rather than holding it low. The changeover occurs where \(\rho(W)=\rho(W_{\mathrm{orig}})\).

In the \textit{C.\ elegans} sign sweeps, this changeover can occur slightly before the empirical negative-edge fraction rather than exactly at it. The reason is that the sweep does not only change the global fraction of negative edges; it also randomly reassigns which edges carry those signs. The shuffle controls show that randomizing sign placement can itself increase performance and CV, consistent with a reduction in \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) (Figure~\ref{fig:shuf_all}). A quick comparison of the sign sweeps in the \textit{C.\ elegans} and ER-matched networks is given in Supplemental Figure~\ref{fig:sign_sweep_quicklook}. Therefore, the perturbed \textit{C.\ elegans} matrix can reach \(\rho(W)<\rho(W_{\mathrm{orig}})\) before the sweep reaches the empirical sign fraction. This offset is not expected to appear as strongly in the ER-matched network, where signs are already effectively random with respect to the topology and reshuffling sign placement has little additional effect on the raw spectral radius.

This pattern suggests that the performance gain from sign balance is mostly mediated by spectral-radius scaling. The benefit is not only that positive and negative edges create a different signed topology; it is also that this signed topology lowers the raw spectral radius, so own-radius normalization applies a larger scale factor. That higher effective recurrent scale improves task-agnostic performance but also makes the reservoir more hyperparameter-sensitive. This interpretation is consistent with recent reservoir-computing work on the no-strong-loops principle: Hadaeghi and colleagues show that stronger reciprocal loops inflate \(\rho(W)\), forcing spectral-radius normalization to downscale recurrent weights more strongly and reducing memory capacity and kernel rank \parencite{hadaeghi_no_strong_loops_2025}. Their manipulation targets reciprocity rather than E/I edge signs, but the mechanism is complementary. Strong loops raise the raw spectral radius and suppress effective recurrent scale after normalization; balanced edge signs can lower the raw spectral radius and permit larger effective recurrent scale.

\subsection{Shuffle controls implicate raw spectral radius as a common mediator}
\label{sec:shuf}
The sign-balance result explains the main architecture-level separation, but it does not explain differences among controls that share the same global sign ratio. The degree-preserving shuffle experiments show that these residual differences follow the same spectral-radius mechanism observed in the sign-fraction experiments. Let \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) denote the spectral radius of the perturbed matrix before it is rescaled to the target value. Across the shuffle controls, decreases in \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) are associated with movement toward higher mean performance and higher CV, whereas increases in \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) are associated with movement toward lower mean performance and lower CV.

With the original real-valued \textit{C.\ elegans} weights, weight shuffling, connection shuffling, and connection-plus-weight shuffling all move the reservoir away from the unshuffled empirical connectome toward higher mean performance and higher CV (Figure~\ref{fig:shuf_all}, bottom row). The color-coded contours show that these controls also have lower raw spectral radius than the empirical baseline. This is the same direction seen in the sign-balance ablation: lowering \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) makes the own-radius normalization apply a larger scale factor, increasing the effective recurrent weights after normalization and producing the performance--invariance tradeoff described above.

The ordering of the real-weight shuffle controls is still informative. In a connection shuffle, weights move with their original outgoing edge source as edges are reassigned to new targets. In a weight shuffle, magnitudes are instead redistributed across the fixed empirical edge set, so source-specific patterns of outgoing synaptic strength are broken. The connection-plus-weight shuffle first disrupts the neighborhood structure and then breaks this source-associated magnitude pattern. Its lower performance and CV relative to the pure connection shuffle indicate that the implicit weight movement caused by connection shuffling is not equivalent to an unconstrained weight shuffle. Keeping heterogeneous weights tied to their original source neuron produces a reservoir with lower \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) than randomizing those magnitudes independently. The smaller differences in CV, performance, and spectral radius between the weight-shuffle and connection-plus-weight-shuffle controls suggest that, within this real-weight setting, the effect of topology alone is limited.

When all nonzero weights are collapsed to \(\pm 1\) while preserving the empirical sign ratio, the direction reverses (Figure~\ref{fig:shuf_all}, middle row). Using the original local sign placement as the baseline, the sign- and topology-shuffled signed-unit controls have lower CV across all four metrics (Supplemental Table~\ref{tab:shuffle_cv_pm1}). These controls also have higher \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\), and hence lower performance and CV than the signed-unit baseline. Thus, preserving only the global sign ratio recovers a lower-variance regime, but it does not make local sign placement irrelevant: the original local sign arrangement lowers the raw spectral radius, placing the signed-unit reservoir in a higher-performance, higher-CV regime than the shuffled signed-unit controls.

The binary-weight experiment isolates topology further. Once every nonzero edge has the same positive sign and magnitude, degree-preserving rewiring only weakly changes \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) and correspondingly produces smaller shifts in the performance--CV plane (Figure~\ref{fig:shuf_all}, top row; Supplemental Table~\ref{tab:shuffle_cv_binary}). This supports the interpretation that topology matters, but that its effect on the tradeoff is strongest when it changes the raw spectrum of the recurrent matrix.

Together, the shuffle experiments make the hierarchy more mechanistic. Global E/I sign imbalance, local sign placement, topology, and heterogeneous magnitude placement are not independent explanations for the performance--invariance tradeoff. They are interdependent structural manipulations that shift \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) before normalization. The direction of movement in the performance--CV plane is then largely predicted by whether that raw spectral radius decreases or increases relative to the relevant baseline.

\subsection{Raw spectral radius summarizes the performance--invariance tradeoff}
\label{sec:raw_rho_summary}
The sign-normalization and shuffle results point to the same mechanism: structural perturbations change the raw recurrent spectrum before spectral-radius normalization, and this changes the effective recurrent scale after normalization. Figure~\ref{fig:raw_rho_summary} summarizes this relationship across the sign-fraction sweeps and the shuffle controls. Across metrics, lower \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) is generally associated with higher mean performance and higher CV, while higher \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) is associated with lower performance and lower CV.

This summary should not be read as saying that raw spectral radius is an independent architectural feature separate from topology, sign placement, or weight magnitudes. Rather, those structural features appear to alter performance by changing \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\). The plotted families show that the same direction appears in multiple experimental settings: balanced sign sweeps, signed-unit controls, binary topology controls, and real-weight shuffles. Thus, the final synthesis of the results is not simply that E/I balance matters, but that E/I balance, topology, and magnitude placement modulate a shared spectral-radius pathway that links reservoir architecture to the performance--invariance tradeoff.

\section{Discussion}
We find a hyperparameter invariance--performance tradeoff dictated by how network structure changes the raw recurrent spectrum before normalization. The \textit{C.\ elegans} connectome occupies a relatively low-variance reservoir regime, and the ablations point to an interwoven hierarchy of causes. Global E/I sign imbalance is the strongest structural perturbation in the model studied here: preserving the empirical sign ratio recovers much of the connectome's invariance even when exact sign placement and empirical weight magnitudes are altered. However, the sign-normalization and shuffle results suggest that the more general mechanism is spectral. Sign balance, local sign placement, topology, and magnitude placement change \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\); spectral-radius normalization then converts those raw spectral differences into different effective recurrent weight scales.

Moving toward a more balanced E/I regime generally increases mean task-agnostic performance, but it also increases CV across spectral radius, leak rate, input scaling, and neuron bias. The same pattern appears in the shuffle controls: manipulations that lower \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) tend to move reservoirs toward higher performance and higher CV, while manipulations that raise \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\) tend to move them toward lower performance and lower CV. Under the present modeling choices, the empirical \textit{C.\ elegans} instantiation lies on the low-variance side of this curve rather than at the peak-performance region. Put more directly, the approximately 4:1 excitatory-to-inhibitory balance associated with many biological networks does not prioritize peak reservoir performance in this abstraction; it helps produce a more hyperparameter-invariant, lower-performance operating point by maintaining a raw spectrum that does not require as much post-normalization amplification.

\subsection{Shape of the sign-balance curves}

The approximate symmetry of the sign-fraction curves around a balanced edge-sign ratio is also consistent with the mathematical form of the reservoir used here. The state nonlinearity is \(\tanh\), which is odd-symmetric, and the readout is linear. Under these choices, exchanging positive recurrent weights for negative recurrent weights changes the sign structure of the recurrent drive, but the linear readout can absorb corresponding sign changes in the state features. This makes the sign-flip experiment approximately symmetric. The centered peak near 50:50 edge signs is therefore consistent with balanced signs suppressing coherent recurrent feedback loops, lowering \(\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}\), and increasing the post-normalization effective recurrent scale. A centered peak in performance and a corresponding increase in CV near balanced edge signs are natural for this abstraction.

This helps explain why the present curves differ from studies in which the best operating point is slightly inhibition-dominated. Srinivasan, Plenz, and Girvan report performance benefits from balanced or slightly inhibition-dominated adaptive E/I regimes, while de Graaf and colleagues use a rectified \(\tanh\)-like activation in a locomotor circuit model \parencite{srinivasan_ei_balance_2025,de_graaf_spinal_inhibition_2025}. In such models, the activation function is not fully sign-symmetric: positive and negative inputs are transformed differently, so the optimal E/I ratio can shift away from exactly 50:50. By contrast, the odd activation and linear readout used here make the sign-flipping experiment more symmetric by construction. The centered shape of the curves should therefore be interpreted as a property of this reservoir abstraction, not as a claim that biological circuits should generally optimize at equal excitation and inhibition.

Direct biological interpretation should be cautious because the connectome model compresses substantial uncertainty into a static signed network. The main analyses use EleganSign-derived signed connectomes, but many biological connections have unknown or complex sign annotations, and the inferred inhibitory edge fraction depends on how those ambiguous cases are handled \parencite{fenyves_szilágyi_zsolt_vassy_csaba_2020,elegansign_web}. We therefore analyze both a signed-only version that removes those ambiguous edges and an augmented version that assigns them random signs under a 4:1 excitatory-to-inhibitory edge prior. If the true inhibitory edge fraction differs from either approximation, the empirical connectome may lie elsewhere along the observed performance--CV curve. In addition, E/I balance varies across development and aging \parencite{wirak_florman_alkema_connor_gabel_2022, tanis_bellemer_moresco_forbush_koelle_2009}, and our model delivers inputs to all neurons rather than to biologically separated populations--such as sending inputs to input neurons and taking outputs from motor neurons. The present work should therefore be read as a structural reservoir analysis, not a faithful dynamical simulation of the animal.

For reservoir computing, the practical implication is that raw spectral radius before normalization should be treated as a key mediator between architecture and performance--invariance behavior. E/I sign ratio remains an important design parameter, complementing recent performance-focused work on adaptive E/I control in reservoirs \parencite{srinivasan_ei_balance_2025}, but its effect should be evaluated through the spectrum it induces together with topology, sign placement, magnitude distribution, and magnitude placement.

\subsection{Future directions}

Several extensions would help clarify these interpretations. First, the model comparisons could be strengthened by adding additional topology controls that match degree and sign statistics more tightly, including degree-matched random and scale-free networks. Such controls would help determine which residual effects are genuinely topological and whether the distinctive sensitivity of MC persists outside the \textit{C.\ elegans} degree distribution.

Second, the present conclusions should be tested on downstream tasks such as NARMA to determine whether the same structural relationships hold when reservoirs are evaluated by task performance rather than task-agnostic statistics alone. The same framework could also be used to investigate how connectome optimization algorithms \parencite{churchland_garcia-ojalvo_2026} alter task-agnostic statistics, and whether task-driven optimization moves biological reservoirs along the same performance--invariance axes identified here. Extending the ablation grid across additional topologies, magnitude distributions, and sign-placement rules would also test how far the relative importance of E/I imbalance generalizes beyond the present connectome-centered setting.

Third, it would be valuable to repeat these experiments with biologically separated input and output populations. Using sensory neurons as inputs and muscles or motor neurons as outputs would bring the model closer to a functional circuit interpretation and would better connect this work to prior studies of connectome-based computation \parencite{KAWAI201915,yu_bpu_2025}. Finally, the role of sign inference deserves more direct study. Natural next steps would be to compare alternative neurotransmitter-to-sign mappings and to repeat the sign-balance analyses under inferred inhibitory fractions closer to those suggested by recent biological work.

\subsection{Neuroscience Terms Explained for Computer Scientists}
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\textbf{E/I balance} -- the ratio or fraction of excitatory to inhibitory synaptic signs in the modeled reservoir; in this paper this is an edge-level quantity, not a count of excitatory or inhibitory neurons}
    \item {\textbf{neurotransmitter} -- a chemical that allows neurons to communicate with each other by transmitting signals across synapses \parencite{Sheffler2023Neurotransmitters}.}
    \item {\textbf{gap junction} -- a type of synapse that connects the cytoplasm of two adjacent cells; bidirectional with the same weight for both sides. In our modeling, these are either removed or treated as two distinct positively signed edges.}
    \item {\textbf{neuromuscular junction} -- a specialized synapse where a motor neuron communicates with a muscle fiber. These directed edges point to non-neural cells and hence are not included in our graph.}
    \item {\textbf{synaptic terminals} -- bulbous endings of a neuron's axon where neurotransmitters are transferred to other cells (neurons or muscles). We use the number of these terminals as a proxy for edge weight.}
    \item {\textbf{membrane potential} -- the difference in electrical charge between the inside and outside of a neuron, forming the basis for neural activity \parencite{Henley_2021}. For our purposes, this is the internal state of the neuron representing how much our activation function will be saturated.}
    \item {\textbf{small-worldness} -- a property commonly observed in biological networks. A graph that exhibits the small-world property has both a high clustering coefficient and a short average path length. This structure is claimed to make information spread more efficient and to make the network more robust \parencite{Bassett2007,latora2001}.}
    \item {\textbf{scale-free network}} -- a network whose degree distribution follows a power law, meaning the fraction of nodes with degree $k$ follows a power law $k^{-\alpha}$ \parencite{broido_clauset_2019}.
\end{itemize}
